# Information and Ideas

About **26% of the Reading and Writing section**. These questions ask what a text *says*, what it *implies*, and which evidence *supports* a claim.

> **Big idea:** the right answer is always supported by the text. If you can't point to the words that prove a choice, it's wrong, however reasonable it sounds.

## Central Ideas and Details {#INI.CID}

**What it tests:** identifying the main idea of a short passage, or a specific detail it states.

**Strategy**
1. Read the whole passage and ask: *what is the author mostly saying?*
2. Watch for pivots like **"however," "yet," "but," "in fact"**. The main idea often comes after them.
3. Predict the answer in your own words **before** looking at the choices.
4. Pick the choice that covers the **whole** passage, not one sentence.

**Common traps**
- **Too narrow:** true detail, but not the main idea.
- **Too broad / extreme:** "always", "all", "the most important", none of which the text supports.
- **Off-topic:** uses words from the passage but says something it doesn't.

## Inferences {#INI.INF}

**What it tests:** completing a passage with the most logical conclusion, usually a sentence ending in a blank.

**Strategy**
1. Find the **evidence** the passage gives (often two facts that must be connected).
2. Ask: *what must be true if these facts are true?*
3. Choose the **least extreme** conclusion that follows directly.

**Example pattern.** "Lizards with larger toe pads grip better. After the hurricane, survivors had larger toe pads. So…": lizards with larger toe pads were more likely to survive. (Not: the storm made toe pads grow.)

**Traps:** conclusions that go further than the evidence, reverse cause and effect, or bring in outside knowledge.

## Command of Evidence {#INI.COE}

**What it tests:** choosing the quotation or data that best **supports** (or weakens) a claim. Questions are either *textual* (a quote from a poem, novel or study) or *quantitative* (a table or graph).

**Strategy for data questions**
1. Read the **claim** first and identify exactly what it says (greatest increase? higher in group A than B? a trend over time?).
2. Check each choice against the table: is it **accurate**, and does it address **that specific claim**?
3. Eliminate choices that are true but irrelevant (they describe a different comparison).

**Strategy for quotation questions**
- Restate the claim in simple words, then find the quote that says the same thing most directly.

**Traps:** an accurate statement about the data that doesn't prove the claim; mixing up years, groups or units.
